AP Physics 1 · Free response

AP Physics 1 practice FRQs that earn every point on the rubric

Free response is half of your AP Physics 1 score, and most students drop marks they already know how to earn: a law left unnamed, a diagram with the wrong arrows, a conclusion that never states the answer. This guide covers what each of the four FRQ types rewards, three original worked examples written the way a full-credit answer reads, and a four-week plan for practising them.

The short answer

On AP Physics 1, a free-response answer is graded as an argument rather than a number. Three habits carry most of the available points.

Name the law before the numbers

Write the principle you are using - Newton's second law for a named system, conservation of momentum for a system with no external impulse - and only then substitute. A substitution with no law above it frequently loses a setup point even when the arithmetic is right.

Every answer is a claim plus evidence

"Greater", "smaller", "stays the same" is a verdict, not an answer. The rubric wants the sentence after it: which quantity in which equation changes, and in which direction, so the verdict follows from physics rather than instinct.

Practise untimed, then timed

Untimed practice is where you build the sentence patterns. Timed practice is where you test whether the patterns survive pressure. Doing only timed practice means you rehearse your current habits instead of replacing them.

What Section II actually asks of you

Four questions, half the score, and the same four skills every year.

Section II of AP Physics 1 contains four free-response questions, and that section contributes half of your total score. A formula sheet and a graphing calculator are provided or allowed, which changes the nature of the test: the exam is not checking whether you can recall the equation for the moment of inertia of a solid disk. It is checking whether you can decide which principle applies to the situation drawn in front of you, connect two or three representations of the same physical event, and justify the conclusion you reach.

That single design decision explains why strong students sometimes score poorly on free response. If you have practised by computing answers, you have practised the part the exam cares about least. The points sit in the setup and the justification, and those are writing skills as much as they are physics skills. The good news is that writing skills respond very well to deliberate practice: the sentence structures are reusable, and once you have them you can spend your thinking on the physics.

It also explains the value of reading rubrics. A scoring rubric is not a mystery document; it is a list of the things a reader must be able to find in your answer. Practising against that list, question after question, is the single highest-yield thing you can do between now and May.

The four free-response types, and the move each one rewards

Same physics, four different jobs. Recognising the job in the first ten seconds saves you from answering the wrong question.

1. Mathematical routines

These ask you to derive or calculate something from the situation: a time, a speed, a force, an energy. The reward is a clean chain of reasoning - state the principle, write it symbolically, substitute with units, then give a final answer with a direction and a sensible number of significant figures. If the question says "derive", do not start from the formula sheet answer; start from the principle and show that the formula follows. A derivation that begins with the result cannot earn the derivation point.

2. Translation between representations

You are given a graph, a diagram, a table or a paragraph and asked to produce a different one: a velocity-time graph from a description of motion, a free-body diagram from a written scenario, an energy bar chart from a sketch of a track. The reward is consistency. Every feature of the new representation must correspond to something in the old one - the sign of the slope, the moment a force changes, the instant a system speeds up. Mark the correspondences explicitly; a correct graph with no explanation often scores lower than a slightly rougher graph that names what each segment represents.

3. Experimental design and analysis

You are handed equipment and asked to determine a quantity, or given data and asked what it shows. The reward is a complete, repeatable procedure plus a stated relationship between the graph and the physics. Naming the equipment is not a design. A design says what you vary, what you hold fixed, what you measure, how many trials, and what you plot so that the graph is linear and the slope means something specific.

4. Qualitative and quantitative translation

This is the type that separates a 4 from a 5. It asks you to move between words, symbols and numbers about the same event - sometimes to predict a direction of change, sometimes to explain why two situations differ. The reward is a stated relationship between two quantities, supported by an equation, and then a conclusion. Vague appeals to "more energy" or "it has more momentum" earn nothing on their own.

Where the points actually go

A typical free-response rubric has five kinds of credit. Knowing them changes what you write first.

Credit What the reader looks for How to make it obvious
Setup A named principle applied to a named system Write "For the system of block plus spring, with no external work done on it, mechanical energy is conserved" as its own sentence.
Representation A diagram, graph or chart that matches the scenario Label every arrow, put a dot on the object, and give axes names and units. Never leave a force arrow unlabelled.
Execution Algebra that follows from the setup Stay symbolic until the last line. Substituting early hides the structure and invites arithmetic slips.
Justification A sentence that connects the result to the physics Finish with "because" and then name the term in the equation that changed. Do not finish with the number alone.
Communication Units, signs, direction, sensible magnitude Answer in the units asked for, state direction in words for vectors, and sanity-check that a cart is not moving at 400 m/s.

The habit that pays: before writing anything, underline the verb. "Derive", "sketch", "justify", "explain", "determine" and "compare" ask for different documents. Most lost free-response points come from answering the verb you expected rather than the verb on the page.

Worked example 1: a mathematical routine, written for full credit

Original question, in the style of an AP Physics 1 kinematics item. Answers are not copied from any released exam.

Question. A student throws a 0.40 kg ball straight upward from a point 1.5 m above the floor. The ball leaves her hand at 12 m/s. Air resistance is negligible and g = 9.8 m/s². (a) Determine the time the ball takes to reach its highest point. (b) Determine the maximum height of the ball above the floor. (c) The ball falls back down and passes the release point. Determine its speed there, and justify your answer without using the time.

(a) Time to the top. Take upward as positive. At the highest point the vertical velocity is zero, so using v = v₀ + at with a = −g:

0 = 12 − 9.8t  →  t = 12 / 9.8 = 1.2 s.

One point for naming constant acceleration with a = −g, one for the algebra, one for the value with a unit. A bare "1.2" with no setup is the most common way to lose the first of those.

(b) Maximum height above the floor. The rise above the release point comes from v² = v₀² + 2aΔy, again with zero velocity at the top:

0 = (12)² − 2(9.8)Δy  →  Δy = 144 / 19.6 = 7.3 m.

The question asks for height above the floor, so add the release height: 7.3 + 1.5 = 8.8 m. That addition is a communication point, and it is the one students forget most often, because the interesting number is the rise rather than the total.

(c) Speed back at the release point. The ball returns to the same height with the same speed, 12 m/s, now directed downward. The justification the rubric wants is an energy statement rather than an appeal to symmetry: the only force doing work is gravity, which is conservative, so the mechanical energy at the release point equals the mechanical energy on the way back through that point. Heights cancel, so ½mv² equals ½mv₀² and the speed is unchanged. The direction reverses; the speed does not. Writing "by symmetry" alone usually earns the answer point but not the justification point.

Worked example 2: energy and momentum in one question

The collision-then-spring pattern appears constantly, because it forces you to switch principles mid-question.

Question. A 0.20 kg dart travelling horizontally at 15 m/s embeds itself in a 1.8 kg wooden block resting on a frictionless horizontal surface. The block is attached to a spring of constant 250 N/m. (a) Determine the speed of the block and dart immediately after the collision. (b) Determine the maximum compression of the spring. (c) Determine how much mechanical energy is lost in the collision, and explain where it went.

(a) After the collision. During the very short collision, the spring has not yet compressed appreciably, so there is no external horizontal impulse on the dart-block system and momentum is conserved:

(0.20)(15) = (0.20 + 1.8)v  →  3.0 = 2.0v  →  v = 1.5 m/s.

(b) Maximum compression. Now the system is the block, dart and spring, and the surface is frictionless, so mechanical energy is conserved from just after the collision to the moment the block stops:

½(2.0)(1.5)² = ½(250)x²  →  2.25 = 125x²  →  x² = 0.018  →  x = 0.13 m.

(c) Energy lost in the collision. Before the collision the only kinetic energy belongs to the dart: ½(0.20)(15)² = 22.5 J. Immediately after, the system has 2.25 J. The difference, 20.3 J, left the mechanical account during the collision, mostly as internal energy and sound in the wood as the dart came to rest relative to the block. A full-credit answer names the mechanism; "some energy was lost" is not an explanation.

Why this question is worth practising. It contains the two traps that appear in almost every momentum FRQ. The first is using energy conservation across the collision, which is false because the collision is inelastic. The second is using momentum conservation across the spring compression, which is false because the wall exerts an external force through the spring. Two principles, two intervals, and the interval boundary is the only place you are allowed to switch.

Worked example 3: the qualitative-quantitative translation

One question, two objects, and a comparison that needs an equation behind it.

Question. A solid disk and a thin hoop have the same mass and the same radius. Both are released from rest at the same height on the same incline and roll without slipping to the bottom. Which object reaches the bottom with the greater speed? Justify your answer with an energy argument, and state the ratio of the two speeds.

Answer. The disk reaches the bottom faster. For rolling without slipping, the kinetic energy at the bottom is split between translation and rotation, and the rotational share depends on the shape through I = cmR²:

mgh = ½mv² + ½Iω² = ½mv² + ½(cmR²)(v²/R²) = ½mv²(1 + c).

So v² = 2gh / (1 + c). The shape with the smaller c converts more of the available gravitational energy into translation, and therefore ends with the larger speed. For a solid disk c = ½, giving v = √(4gh/3); for a hoop c = 1, giving v = √(gh). The ratio is v_disk / v_hoop = √(4/3) ≈ 1.15, so the disk is about 15 percent faster at the bottom.

Notice what makes this a full-credit answer rather than a guess. It states a verdict, gives the general relationship that produces the verdict, identifies which parameter differs between the two objects, and then quantifies the difference. The same four moves work for every comparison question on the exam, whether the quantities compared are speeds, periods, forces or pressures.

How to write an experimental design answer that scores

Design questions look open-ended and are actually the most templated item on the paper. Four elements, in this order.

What you vary, and what you hold fixed. Say it explicitly: "Vary the hanging mass m while keeping the spring and the amplitude the same." A design that changes two things at once cannot support a claim about either, and readers are trained to look for that sentence.

What you measure, and with what. Name the instrument and the quantity, not the brand. "Measure the period T with a photogate for each mass" is specific. "Use the equipment to find the period" is not. Mention repeats and averaging, because a single trial does not establish a trend.

What you plot, and why that makes the relationship linear. This is where most answers stop short. If theory predicts T = 2π√(m/k), plotting T against m gives a curve that is hard to test. Plot T² against m and the prediction is a straight line through the origin with slope 4π²/k, so the value of k comes from the slope rather than from one data point.

How you would know you were wrong. A strong answer includes a failure condition: what the graph would look like if the model did not hold, or which systematic error would shift the intercept away from the origin. That sentence is cheap to write and it demonstrates the reasoning the rubric is asking for.

The four mistakes that cost the most points

These four account for a large share of the gap between what students know and what they score.

What students often write

  • A number with no principle above it.
  • A free-body diagram with unlabelled arrows, or a force drawn on the wrong object.
  • Energy conservation applied across an inelastic collision.
  • "It increases because there is more force" with no equation named.
  • An experimental design that names equipment but no graph.

What the rubric is looking for

  • The principle, the system, then the substitution.
  • Every arrow labelled, every force attributable to something touching the object.
  • Momentum across the collision, energy across the interval after it.
  • The relationship between two quantities, quoted from an equation, then the verdict.
  • A linearised graph whose slope equals the quantity you want.

One more: answering a question you were not asked. If the prompt says "compare", a correct number for one object earns partial credit at best. Underline the verb before you start writing, every single time.

A four-week AP Physics 1 FRQ practice plan

Thirty to forty minutes a day, five days a week. Untimed first, timed later, and always against a rubric.

Week 1 - build the sentences

Choose one FRQ per day from kinematics, forces, energy and momentum. Work untimed. After each answer, write the rubric points you think you earned, then check them. Keep a list of the sentence patterns that scored: "for the system of …, the net external force is …, therefore …".

Week 2 - representations

Do translation questions only: graph to description, description to free-body diagram, diagram to energy bar chart. Redraw each figure from scratch rather than annotating the printed one. The rebuild is where the representation habit forms.

Week 3 - design and rotation

One experimental design question a day, plus torque and rotational dynamics, which is where most students have the least practice and the most available points. Write the linearisation explicitly every time, even when it feels repetitive.

Week 4 - timed and mixed

Do full four-question sets on the clock, in one sitting, with the formula sheet and calculator you will actually use. Mark them against rubrics the same day. Then redo, from a blank page, every question you scored below two-thirds on.

Every day - one redo

Redo yesterday's worst answer before starting today's new one. Rewriting a weak answer into a full-credit one teaches more than attempting a fresh question, because the diagnosis is already done.

Every week - one read

Read one scoring rubric for a question you have not attempted, and predict what a full-credit answer would contain. Then attempt it. Testing your model of the rubric is the fastest way to fix it.

AP Physics 1 free response, answered

How many FRQs are on the AP Physics 1 exam?

Section II of AP Physics 1 has four free-response questions and is worth half of your total score. The four questions are normally one mathematical routine, one translation between representations, one experimental design and analysis, and one qualitative-quantitative translation, though the order varies from year to year.

Are calculators allowed on AP Physics 1 free response?

Yes. A graphing calculator is allowed on AP Physics 1, and a formula sheet is provided with the exam. The calculator is for arithmetic and for checking a graph shape, not for replacing the setup: the rubric awards the law you name and the quantities you connect before it awards a number.

How long should I spend on each AP Physics 1 FRQ?

Treat the four questions as roughly equal in time and keep the last five minutes of Section II for units, signs and any part you skipped. If a question has six labelled parts, answer the short qualitative parts first, because they are usually worth one point each and take seconds.

Where can I find AP Physics 1 practice FRQs for free?

Released free-response questions from past exams through College Board are the standard source, and AP Classroom has additional practice. Veelearn adds the part that released questions do not give you: a written lesson for each unit, worked examples at the difficulty of the question you are about to attempt, and feedback on the answer you actually wrote.

Do I need to memorise formulas for AP Physics 1?

You do not need to memorise the catalog formulas, because the formula sheet is provided. You do need to know which formula applies to the system you drew, what each symbol means, and under what condition the equation is valid - that selection step is what the free-response rubric is really testing.
